## 1. Regarding infrastructure operations, have you done any business operations / deployment of business?
### Standard Answer

My previous work did lean toward infrastructure, but during actual operations, I did have exposure to business deployment and runtime assurance.

For example, in a containerized environment, I participated in application deployment, configuration adjustments, and post-deployment runtime observation and issue troubleshooting.

In this process, I not only focused on whether resources were normal, but also on whether the business was available, such as interface access, log anomalies, and whether service dependencies were normal.

My understanding is that infrastructure operations mainly provide capabilities, while business operations ensure business runtime. In my actual work, I gradually moved toward this direction.

---

## 2. How to build the connection between development and operations? How to establish standards for K8s business releases?
### Standard Answer

My understanding is that development and operations need to connect through "standardization" rather than personal experience.

This can be done from several aspects:

First, standardize the release process, unify construction, testing, and release through CI/CD processes, reducing uncertainty from manual operations.

Second, image specifications, image tags should not use simple v1, v2, but instead bind with git commit or pipeline ID to ensure traceability and facilitate rollback.

Third, environment stratification, releases must pass pre-release environment validation to reduce direct release risks.

Fourth, monitoring and alerting systems, to promptly perceive business status after release, rather than relying on user feedback.

Fifth, operational documentation and operation specifications, solidify release, rollback, and troubleshooting processes to ensure consistent execution results across different personnel.

The overall goal is to transform the collaboration between development and operations into a controllable and reusable system through processes, specifications, and tools.

---

## 3. How to govern a chaotic new environment?
### Standard Answer

If entering an environment with many historical legacy issues, I would not start with comprehensive transformation, but instead proceed in stages.

First, asset inventory, establish a basic ledger, clarify existing resource status, and clean up redundant and unattended resources.

Second, clarify business ownership, clearly define system owners and contact persons to solve the problem of unclear responsibilities.

Third, clarify business and middleware dependencies, such as front-end, back-end, message queues, and databases, and based on this, converge permissions.

Fourth, converge infrastructure management capabilities, such as establishing a management network, incorporating devices into unified management, and improving overall controllability.

Fifth, establish a monitoring and alerting system to enable timely issue detection and clear responsibility for response.

Sixth, experience consolidation, including knowledge base, incident post-mortems, and SOPs, to solidify experience.

The overall approach is: from chaos → visible → controllable → standardized, rather than doing everything at once.

## Case One: Service is Normal, Pod is Normal, but Business Access is Abnormal

### Applicable Questions

- Have you ever had real troubleshooting experience?
- When Service is normal but business is inaccessible, how would you investigate?
- How would you determine if it's a business issue or a platform issue?

### Standard Answer

I once encountered a typical case:

From the Kubernetes resource perspective, the Pod was Running, the Service was normal, and Endpoints were present, but business access was still abnormal.

At the time, my troubleshooting approach was to divide it into layers.

First, confirm Pod status, Service, and Endpoints to ensure the basic forwarding chain was established.

Second, validate the business itself by curling the Service's ClusterIP and port to check if the application layer was returning an error.

Third, check Nginx configuration and the actual effective configuration files inside the container, eventually finding that the issue wasn't with the K8s resources themselves, but rather the ConfigMap's business configuration and the expected access path were inconsistent.

The root cause was that after the application configuration was changed, although the ConfigMap was modified, the Pod wasn't rebuilt, leading to the existing containers still using the old configuration, hence the business abnormality.

In subsequent handling, I re-triggered the Deployment rolling update to ensure the new configuration took effect, and also made an experience summary:

For ConfigMap-type configuration changes, it's not enough to just modify the resource object; you must confirm whether the change was actually applied to the running instance, and after release, add business-side validation, rather than just checking Pod status.

### Extractable Capabilities

- Understanding that "resource normal ≠ business normal"
- Having a layered troubleshooting mindset
- Ability to solidify lessons into release check items

### One-Sentence Summary Version

This was a typical case of "platform layer normal, business layer abnormal," where I eventually traced it to the ConfigMap change not triggering business instance updates. I later realized that post-release validation shouldn't only check K8s resource status but also include business-side validation.

# Step 1: Confirm that the Harbor repository address, certificate, and login method are functioning normally;

# Step 2: Check the containerd registry configuration and certificate directory on the node side;

# Step 3: Manually pull the image using crictl to narrow down the issue scope.

The root cause was that containerd was not reading the registry certificate directory and configuration path as expected. The previously used configuration method did not take effect, so the node could still not trust the Harbor repository.

The resolution was to adjust the containerd registry configuration path according to the standard method, confirm that the certs.d directory structure, domain name, and certificate location match, then restart containerd and re-validate the image pull success.

This experience formed a clear approach for me later:

Such issues cannot be judged solely by whether the repository can be opened. They must be validated from multiple dimensions including the repository end, node runtime, certificate trust chain, and actual pull commands.

Additionally, if writing operation documentation in the future, configuration change-related issues must require "backup first, modify according to standard paths, and validate pull after modification". It's not enough to just explain the principle.

### Extractable Capabilities

- Ability to troubleshoot runtime / repository / certificate chain issues
- Understanding of the importance of standardized operations
- Ability to convert issues into subsequent SOP check items

### One-sentence Summary

This was a typical release chain issue. On the surface, it looked like a repository problem, but in reality, the node-side containerd did not correctly apply the Harbor certificate and registry configuration. Finally, it was resolved through standardized modifications and validation.

## Case 3: Configuration Change / After Release, Business Did Not Update as Expected

### Applicable Issues

- Have you participated in a release?
- How do you understand release standards?
- Why are pre-release and post-release validation necessary?

### Standard Answer

I've encountered a typical release issue:

The configuration was changed, resources were re-applied, but the business behavior did not change as expected.

Such issues often lead people to mistakenly believe that "the configuration was successfully released", but the actual runtime result was not the case.

My approach at the time was to distinguish between "whether the configuration object was successfully changed" and "whether the business instance actually loaded the new configuration".

During troubleshooting, I first checked if the ConfigMap or Deployment resource objects were updated, then verified if the Pod completed a rolling update, and if necessary, entered the container to validate the actual loaded configuration content.

The root cause was identified as: the configuration layer changed, but the runtime instance layer did not achieve consistent updates, leading to the business still using the old logic.

Later, I categorized this as a release standard issue rather than just a technical problem.

Because if the release process lacks "instance rebuild confirmation", "business validation", and "clear rollback points", it's easy to encounter "surface success, actual failure".

My current understanding of releases is:

Release is not just executing commands. It should at least include version traceability, configuration effectiveness validation, business validation, and rollback plans when necessary.

### Extractable Capabilities

- Understanding that release is more than just applying
- Recognizing the importance of post-release validation
- Ability to elevate a single incident to process optimization

### One-sentence Summary

This incident made me realize that a successful release cannot be judged solely by whether the resource objects were updated. It's also necessary to confirm whether the configuration was actually applied to the runtime instance and whether business validation passed.

## Section 5: General Template for Case Answers

### 1. Describe the phenomenon first

For example:

- Pods and Services appear normal, but business access is abnormal
- Image pull failure
- Business did not update after configuration change

### 2. Explain the impact

For example:

- Affects business access
- Affects release process
- Affects configuration deployment efficiency

### 3. Describe the troubleshooting process

Always demonstrate a "layered approach":

- Resource layer
- Configuration layer
- Application layer
- Runtime / network layer

### 4. Explain the root cause

Do not just say "the issue was resolved". Instead, say:

- Configuration was not effective
- Container was not rebuilt
- Registry / certs configuration was not standardized

### 5. Finally, discuss the optimization

This is what quality roles love to hear:

- Add release check items
- Add business validation
- Document operational procedures
- Standardize configuration modification processes
